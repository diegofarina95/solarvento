How to Read Your Electricity Bill in Spain, Step by Step

7 July 2026

Grab your latest electricity bill and put it next to you. We’re going to go through it from top to bottom, in the same order the items appear, until there isn’t a single field you don’t understand.

Bills vary a little depending on the electricity retailer, but they all include the same mandatory information in a similar order. If you follow these six steps on your own bill, you’ll be able to read any of them.

Step 1 · The header: who, when and how much

Right at the top, almost always highlighted in a box, you’ll find the basics: the total amount, the invoice number, the issue date and the billing period (for example, “from 1 to 30 April”).

Look closely at the period: it tells you how many days this bill covers. Not all bills cover exactly one month, and that explains why one can sometimes look more expensive than another even if your usage was similar: it covers more days.

Step 2 · The contract details

This is a block, usually on the first page, with the technical details of your supply. The ones that really matter are:

What to look for in the contract details

  1. Account holder and supply address — who and where. Careful: it may be different from the mailing address.
  2. CUPS — the unique code for your supply point, starting with “ES”. It’s the ID number of your electricity connection: it doesn’t change even if you switch company.
  3. Access tariff / rate (peaje de acceso) — almost always “2.0TD” for homes. It defines how your electricity is charged by time of day.
  4. Contracted power (potencia contratada) — in kW, sometimes two values: peak (punta) and off-peak (valle). The maximum you can use at the same time.
Keep your CUPS handy: it’s the detail you’ll be asked for when comparing offers or switching company. Having it nearby saves you phone calls.

Step 3 · Power: what you pay to stay connected

Here’s where the money starts. The fixed power charge (término de potencia) is a fixed fee: you pay to have those kW available, whether you use them or not. It’s calculated by multiplying your contracted power by a price per kW and by the number of days in the period.

How to know if you’re overpaying: if your breaker never trips when you plug in several things at once (oven + hob + washing machine), you probably have more power than you need and could lower it.

Step 4 · Consumption by time period

With the 2.0TD rate, your consumption is split into three time bands, each with a different price:

  • Peak (P1): the most expensive. Mornings (10:00–14:00) and evenings (18:00–22:00), Monday to Friday.
  • Standard (llano) (P2): mid-priced. The remaining hours on working days.
  • Off-peak (P3): the cheapest. Nights (00:00–08:00), weekends and public holidays.

The bill tells you how many kWh you used in each time band. This split is the most revealing part: if you see a lot of consumption during peak hours, that’s where you have room to save by moving the washing machine, dishwasher or car charging to night-time or the weekend.

Step 5 · The energy charge (término de energía)

This is the big line item and the one that truly depends on you: the kWh consumed multiplied by their price in each period. It’s what goes up when you switch more things on and goes down when you save energy.

If your contract has a fixed price, the kWh price stays constant throughout the period. If it’s indexed (PVPC or similar), it changes every hour according to the market — which is why two months with the same consumption can cost different amounts.

Step 6 · Taxes, meter rental and total

At the end, the items you can’t change are added on:

The final section of the bill

  1. Electricity tax — a percentage applied to energy + power. Regulated, the same for everyone.
  2. Meter rental — the measuring equipment. A few cents per day.
  3. VAT — applied to everything above. The rate may vary depending on current regulations.
  4. Total bill — the sum of everything. The number that ends up leaving your bank account.
A quick recap: header (how much and which period), contract (CUPS and power), power (fixed fee), consumption by time period (where you spend), energy (the variable part) and taxes (the unavoidable bit). With that, you now know how to read any bill.

Now that you understand it

Reading your bill is the first step towards spending less: once you know where your money is going, you can adjust your contracted power, move consumption to off-peak hours or consider bigger changes. If your consumption is high and a good share happens during the day, you may want to see how much you could save with solar panels — but that’s another story. For now, knowing how to read your bill already puts you in a much stronger position.
