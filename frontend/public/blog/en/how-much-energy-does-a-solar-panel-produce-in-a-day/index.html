How Much Energy Does a Solar Panel Really Produce in a Day?

8 July 2026

You look at a panel on a roof and it seems to always do the same thing: sit there, still, in the sun. But inside, its production rises and falls constantly throughout the day, drawing a curve. Understanding that curve explains almost everything else about solar self-consumption: why it pays off, when it pays off, and why timing matters as much as quantity.

Let’s follow a panel from sunrise to sunset, without formulas, to see what it really produces.

The arc of the day: the panel draws a mountain

A solar panel does not produce a fixed amount. Its generation follows the sun: it starts at almost zero at dawn, rises as the sun gets higher, reaches its maximum around solar noon —when the sun is at its highest— and then drops again until it switches off at dusk. If you drew it, it would look like a mountain: gentle on the sides, with a peak in the middle of the day.

This has a huge consequence: most of the day’s energy is concentrated in a few central hours. The first and last hours of sunlight contribute little. That’s why what you do with that midday energy is what determines whether your system performs well.

The key idea: a panel does not spread its production evenly throughout the day. It concentrates most of it around midday. If you consume at that time, you use it; if not, you export it to the grid cheaply. That is where most of the solar self-consumption game is played.

And how much is “a lot”? Putting numbers on it

A typical residential panel today is around 450 W of power. That figure is its theoretical maximum, under ideal conditions: perpendicular sun, clear sky, mild temperature. In real life it almost always produces less, because the sun is not perpendicular all day and the sky is not always clear.

Over the course of a sunny day in Spain, a single panel can generate around 1.5–2.5 kWh, depending on the time of year and the area. To give you an idea of what that means:

What you can do with what ONE panel produces on a good day

  1. ≈ 2 kWh on a sunny day — the approximate output of a 450 W panel. It varies a lot between a July day and a January day.
  2. A couple of washing machine cycles — or a dishwasher and a bit more. The day’s “heavy” appliances.
  3. About 10–12 km in an electric car — energy converted into kilometres. One panel per day, a short drive.
  4. Hours and hours of fridge, lights and electronics — “light” consumption barely makes a dent. Here, there is energy to spare.

A solar installation does not have just one panel, of course, but several —ten, fifteen, twenty—. Multiply those figures and you can see why a roof can cover a large share of a home’s consumption… as long as that consumption lines up with the hours of sunlight.

Why January and July are worlds apart

The same panel, on the same roof, produces much more in summer than in winter. It is not just that there are more daylight hours: in summer the sun climbs higher and its rays arrive more directly, while in winter it stays low and hits at an angle. The difference between a July day and a December day can be more than double.

That is why solar self-consumption has a curious asymmetry: in summer you will probably have surplus energy at midday, and in winter —just when you use more artificial light and heating— production is at its lowest. Understanding this prevents surprises: panels do not “cover” winter in the same way as summer.

An honest nuance: clouds matter, a lot. A panel still produces under overcast skies, but much less. The figures “on a good day” are exactly that: for a good day. The real annual average is lower, and any serious estimate takes that into account.

From curiosity to calculation

All this physics —the arc of the day, seasonality, clouds— is exactly what separates a serious estimate from a rough guess. Saying “I have ten 450 W panels, so I produce X” ignores the fact that production depends on your latitude, your roof’s orientation, the season and the climate in your area.

Solar radiation data by location exists and is public: in Europe it is published by the European Commission’s PVGIS, which is the basis for reliable estimates. If you are curious about how much a roof like yours would really produce, in your specific area, you can calculate it with your location and your electricity bill and see the curve for your own case.

In summary

A panel does not produce at a constant rate: it draws a mountain with a peak at midday, generates more in summer than in winter, and clouds reduce its output. A single panel can produce around 2 kWh on a good day —a couple of washing machine cycles or a few kilometres in an electric car—. What determines whether that is useful to you is not only how much it produces, but whether you are there to make use of it when it produces it.
