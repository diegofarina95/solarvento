Which appliances drive up your electricity use (and how much each costs per year)

7 July 2026

Not every appliance at home weighs the same on your bill. A handful account for most of your consumption, while the rest barely make a dent. Knowing which ones drive up your costs is the fastest way to cut back without giving up what matters.

If you’ve already read how your electricity bill is broken down, you know that the energy term (término de energía) —the kWh you consume— is the part you can influence. This article goes one step further: where do those kWh come from? Let’s look at which appliances are the biggest culprits, how much each one costs per year and what you can do about each of them.

Why some appliances cost so much and others almost nothing

There’s one principle that explains almost your entire bill: what costs money is producing heat or cold. Heating water, heating your home in winter, cooling it in summer or keeping food cold 24 hours a day. Any appliance whose job is to change the temperature of something uses far more electricity than an electronic device.

The reason is physics: heating a litre of water or raising a room’s temperature by two degrees takes a lot of energy. By contrast, a TV, a laptop or an LED bulb uses very little even if you leave it on for hours. That’s why the myth of “unplug your phone charger to save money” is almost anecdotal: the charger uses a tiny fraction of what your water heater uses for one shower.

The key idea: if you want to lower your bill, don’t start by turning off the router or unplugging chargers. Start with what heats or cools. That’s where 80% of the money is.

The biggest consumers, ranked from highest to lowest

These are indicative figures for an average household, using a reference price of around €0.15/kWh. Your case will vary depending on the model, usage, your home’s insulation and your tariff, but the order of magnitude and the ranking hold true in almost every home:

Approximate annual consumption by appliance

  1. Electric heating — 1,500 to 3,000 kWh/year — by far the biggest expense in winter. €225–€450 per year. Radiators, storage heaters or air conditioning in heat mode.
  2. Electric water heater (hot water) — 1,000 to 2,500 kWh/year — heating water is very expensive. €150–€375 per year. One of the biggest potential savings.
  3. Air conditioning — 400 to 1,000 kWh/year — depending on climate and hours of use. €60–€150 per year, concentrated in summer.
  4. Fridge — 250 to 500 kWh/year — low per hour, but it is always on. €40–€75 per year. Much more for older models.
  5. Washing machine, dishwasher and tumble dryer — 200 to 600 kWh/year — mainly due to heating water. €30–€90 per year, depending on how many cycles you run.
  6. Oven and ceramic hob — 200 to 500 kWh/year — high peaks but short use. €30–€75 per year.
  7. Standby and small appliances — 100 to 300 kWh/year — the drip-drip of everything left plugged in. €15–€45 per year. Real, but not where the bulk of the cost is.

Notice the jump: the first item on the list can cost ten times more than the last. That’s why it makes so little sense to obsess over standby losses while ignoring a poorly programmed water heater.

What to do with each of the big ones

As you can see, heating, hot water and climate control take the lion’s share. Let’s go one by one, because each has its own trick.

Heating: winter’s number one

Lowering the thermostat by just one degree reduces consumption noticeably —around 7% per degree, according to most estimates—. Programming it so it doesn’t heat an empty home while you’re at work is another big saving: there’s no point heating the living room at 11 in the morning if you won’t be back until 6 p.m.

And there’s a bigger point: direct electric radiators are among the most expensive heating systems there are. If you’re considering a medium-term change, a heat pump or aerothermal system delivers two or three times more heat for every kWh it consumes, so in the long run it can significantly cut this part of the bill.

Hot water: the invisible expense

An electric water heater uses a lot because heating water requires a lot of energy, and it often does it at any time of day. Two simple adjustments: program it to heat during off-peak hours (horas valle), at night, when electricity is cheaper, and lower its temperature to 55–60 ºC. Above that, you don’t gain comfort —you end up mixing it with cold water anyway— but you do spend more and accelerate limescale build-up.

If your water heater is old or too small and keeps running short, sometimes the problem is not that it uses too little, but that you have it reheating water all day. A correctly sized and well-programmed water heater is one of the most rewarding savings.

Climate control: every degree counts twice

In summer and winter, every extra degree pushes consumption up, because the appliance has to work harder against the temperature difference with the outside. The recommended balance between comfort and cost is usually around 21 ºC in winter and 26 ºC in summer. Using automatic mode and cleaning the filters —a dirty filter makes the unit consume more to do the same job— completes the easy savings.

Fridge: small but constant

It doesn’t use much per hour, but it never turns off, so it adds up. The keys: don’t place it right next to the oven or another heat source, keep it at around 4–5 ºC (no colder than necessary), and don’t leave the door open while deciding what to take out. If yours is more than 12–15 years old, a modern efficient-class model can use less than half as much.

And timing matters as much as the appliance: many of these loads —water heater, washing machine, dishwasher, electric car charging— can be shifted to off-peak hours, when electricity is cheaper. Same consumption, fewer euros, just by choosing the right time.

Reducing consumption is half the story; the other half is where the energy comes from

Everything above is about using less. But there’s a second lever: changing where the electricity you do need comes from. And this is where the profile of your biggest consumers matters.

If your biggest expenses mainly run during the day —air conditioning during sunny hours, a heat pump, appliances you switch on in the afternoon— you create demand exactly when solar panels would be generating. That overlap is what makes self-consumption pay off: you use your own energy at the moment it is produced, instead of buying it from the grid.

If, on the other hand, almost all your consumption is at night, solar without a battery will go less far. That’s why it’s not a matter of “the more panels the better”, but of matching your consumption profile with what the sun can give you.

The way to clear up the doubt is not to do rough calculations, but to look at your specific case using your own consumption data.

In summary

Most of your bill comes from a handful of appliances that produce heat or cold: heating, hot water and climate control, in that order. Tackle those three —by lowering temperatures, programming them for off-peak hours and, when it makes sense, switching to more efficient systems— and you’ll barely need to worry about the drip-drip from the rest.

And if, after seeing the ranking, you’ve realised your consumption is high and a good share of it happens during the day, it may be worth seeing how much you could save with solar in your specific case before ruling it out or taking it for granted.
