Where the electricity from your socket comes from: the journey of a kWh

8 July 2026

You switch on the light and there it is, just like that. You never think about where it comes from, just as you don’t think about where the water from the tap comes from. But every kWh you use has travelled hundreds of kilometres in a fraction of a second, passing through several hands before reaching your light bulb. This is that journey, from start to finish.

Understanding it is not just interesting: it also explains why your electricity bill has the items it has, and why producing your own energy on your roof changes the equation so much.

Stage 1 · Generation: where the kWh is born

Everything starts somewhere electricity is produced. In Spain, that kWh can be born in very different places: a wind farm on a mountain ridge, a solar plant on dry land, a hydroelectric plant at a reservoir, a combined-cycle gas plant, or a nuclear power station. At any time of day, the grid is a changing mix of all those sources.

The interesting thing is that you can’t know “where” the specific kWh from your socket comes from: once it is on the grid, all electricity mixes together. You consume from a shared river fed by many sources at once, and that mix changes every hour depending on how much the wind is blowing, how much the sun is shining and what needs to be switched on to cover the rest.

Stage 2 · Transmission: hundreds of kilometres at high voltage

Electricity is almost never generated where it is consumed. A wind farm is in the countryside; you are in the city. To cover that distance without losing too much energy along the way, it is transported at very high voltage —those huge towers with cables crossing the landscape—.

Why so much voltage: transporting electricity at high voltage loses far less energy than doing it at low voltage. It’s a physics trick: by raising the voltage, the current falls, and with less current the cables heat up less and less energy is wasted along the way. That is why transmission lines run at 400,000 volts and the socket in your home at 230.

Stage 3 · Transformers: lowering the voltage

Those 400,000 volts would be absurd —and extremely dangerous— in your home. So as electricity gets closer to cities and neighbourhoods, it passes through substations and transformers that lower the voltage in stages: from transmission to distribution, and from distribution to the 230 volts that come out of your socket.

That small transformer you may have seen in your street or neighbourhood —a small building or a pole with equipment— is one of the final steps: it converts the neighbourhood’s electricity to the final voltage that enters homes.

Stage 4 · Distribution to your meter

From that final transformer, the electricity, now at low voltage, reaches your building through the distribution grid, goes up through the installation and passes through your meter, which measures every kWh that comes in. That meter provides the data that later appears on your bill.

This whole journey —generation, transmission, transformation, distribution— happens practically instantly. When you flip a switch, you are not waiting for the electricity to “arrive”: the grid is permanently in balance, generating at every moment almost exactly what is being consumed across the whole country at the same time.

Why this journey appears on your bill

This connects with something you may already have seen on your electricity bill. You don’t only pay for the energy itself: you also pay for the maintenance of all that infrastructure —the towers, the substations, the grid— through access tolls (peajes) and regulated charges (cargos regulados). That is why part of your bill is fixed and does not fall even if you consume less: it supports the system that makes the journey possible.

And this is where self-consumption comes in: when you install solar panels on your roof, part of your electricity no longer makes that journey of hundreds of kilometres. It is born on your roof and consumed right there, with no transmission, no transformers, and no losses along the way. It is the shortest route possible: from roof to socket.

In short

Every kWh from your socket is born in a power plant or a renewable park, travels at very high voltage through the transmission grid, has its voltage lowered in substations and transformers, and reaches your meter through distribution — all in a fraction of a second. That journey has a cost, and that is why part of your bill pays for it. The electricity you produce on your own roof skips almost all of that route: it is the kWh that travels the least.
