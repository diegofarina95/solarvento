How Solar Surplus Export to the Grid Works (and What a “Virtual Battery” Is)

15 July 2026

Your panels are producing at full power at midday, when there may be nobody at home using electricity. That leftover energy goes to the grid. And that is when the questions start: do they pay you for it, are you giving it away, and what is this “virtual battery” that energy companies advertise but that turns out not to be a battery at all? Let’s clear it all up without the jargon they often use to confuse you.

What solar surplus is

When your panels produce more electricity than you are using at that exact moment, that leftover energy does not disappear into thin air: it is exported to the electricity grid. That is what we call surplus. Your meter (which is bidirectional, meaning it measures in both directions) records every kWh you export. The question is what you get in return.

Surplus compensation: how they pay you for it

In Spain there is a legal mechanism called simplified surplus compensation (compensación simplificada de excedentes). It works like this: your electricity retailer records the energy you export, assigns it a price (in 2026, roughly between €0.05 and €0.12 per kWh depending on the company and your contract), and deducts that amount from your bill.

But there is an important detail that almost nobody explains properly: it is not the same as selling electricity. It is a discount on your bill, not income paid into your bank account. And it has a crucial limit that is worth understanding.

The limit you need to know: compensation can only discount up to the value of the electricity you consume from the grid that month. In other words, it can bring your energy charge (término de energía) down to zero, but not below that. If one month you produce much more than you use, the surplus above your consumption is NOT paid: it is lost. It is as if you were giving that electricity to the grid for free.

Why the bill never quite reaches zero

Here is another common surprise. Even if you offset all your energy consumption, your bill never reaches zero, because there are items that compensation cannot touch: the contracted power charge (término de potencia), grid charges (peajes), meter rental, and taxes on those items. You still pay all of that even if your panels cover all your energy. Compensation only applies to the energy you have consumed, not to fixed costs.

And this is where the “virtual battery” comes in

The virtual battery was created precisely to solve the problem of surplus energy being lost. And the first thing to say is what it is not:

It is not a battery: a virtual battery is NOT a device and it does NOT store electricity. It is an accounting service offered by your electricity retailer. Instead of storing energy, it stores the economic value of your surplus as a balance in euros — a kind of digital wallet or piggy bank — that you can use later on.

The difference compared with standard compensation is the key:

Simple compensation vs. virtual battery

  1. Simplified compensation — discounts your surplus only on THAT month’s bill, and only until the energy charge reaches zero. Anything above that is lost.
  2. Virtual battery — the value of your surplus builds up as a balance and does NOT expire each month. You keep it for months with less sun, such as winter.
  3. Using the balance — with many virtual batteries, you can use that balance for other bill items or even for another home. For example, paying the bill for your beach house with the sun from your main home.
  4. The result — it allows you to get close to zero-euro bills for several months of the year. You store summer surplus to use it in winter.

The typical example: in summer you produce much more than you use. With standard compensation, that excess would be lost. With a virtual battery, it is stored as a balance and you use it in winter, when you produce little and consume more. That way you make use of almost all your energy over the year.

The honest nuance: self-consuming is always worth more than exporting

And now for the truth that electricity retailers do not highlight quite so much: every kWh you consume directly from your panels is worth more than every kWh you export to the grid. Why? Because when you self-consume, you save the full cost of buying that electricity (€0.15–€0.18/kWh). When you export, they compensate you at a much lower rate (€0.05–€0.12/kWh). The difference is significant.

The right priority: the first goal is to use your own energy at the moment you produce it (running the washing machine, heating water, or charging the car at midday). That is what saves the most. Exporting to the grid and being compensated is plan B for what you cannot self-consume. A virtual battery is a good add-on so you do not lose that surplus, but it does not replace the number-one strategy: use your solar power when you have it.

So, is a virtual battery worth it for me?

It depends on your profile. It is especially useful if you produce much more than you consume (a large solar installation, low daytime consumption), if you have a second home with the same electricity retailer, or if your consumption and production are unbalanced across the seasons. If, on the other hand, you self-consume almost everything you produce, the benefit is smaller. And watch the small print: some providers charge a monthly fee, and the balance is usually lost if you switch electricity retailer, so it is best to use it before you move.

In short

When your panels produce more than you need, that surplus is exported to the grid and your electricity retailer compensates you with a discount on your bill (€0.05–€0.12/kWh), but with a limit: it cannot take your bill below zero or offset fixed costs, and any excess above your consumption is lost. A virtual battery solves this by storing the value of your surplus as a balance in euros — it is not a device, it is a digital wallet — so you can use it in other months or homes. But remember the golden rule: self-consuming your energy in the moment is always worth more than exporting it. Produce it, and use your solar power when you have it.
