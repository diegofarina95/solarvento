How to Read Your Home’s Energy Performance Certificate (and Why It Matters More Than Ever)

14 July 2026

Almost everyone has an energy performance certificate for their home sitting in a drawer — you need one to buy, sell or rent — but very few people really know how to read it. That coloured label with a letter from A to G says a lot about your property: how much it consumes, how much it emits, how much it costs you and how much it is worth on the market. And in 2026 it matters more than ever, because it is linked to tax deductions and to European regulation that is starting to tighten. Let’s learn how to read it.

What it is and what it measures

The energy performance certificate (EPC) is an official document that assesses how much energy your home consumes under normal conditions of use and how much CO2 it emits, all per square metre per year. It is issued by an authorised technician (architect, engineer or technical architect) after visiting the property, and it must be registered with the relevant body in your autonomous community to be legally valid.

It does not measure what you actually spend — that depends on your habits — but how efficient the property itself is: its insulation, windows, heating, cooling and hot water systems. It is like the official fuel consumption figure for a car: a standardised reference that allows comparisons.

The letters: from green A to red G

The heart of the certificate is the label with a letter and a colour, just like on household appliances:

What each letter means

  1. A (dark green) — maximum efficiency, minimum consumption and emissions. Typical of well-built new-build homes.
  2. B (green) — very efficient. The target for many renovation projects.
  3. C and D (yellow) — average efficiency, with room for improvement. Consumption above the ideal level.
  4. E and F (orange) — low efficiency, older systems. Where most of Spain’s housing stock sits.
  5. G (red) — very inefficient, high energy costs. The worst rating; its certificate also expires sooner.

The difference between the two extremes is huge: an A-rated home can consume up to 90% less energy than a G-rated one. And here is a revealing figure for Spain: more than 85% of the residential housing stock is rated E or worse. If your home is second-hand and has not been renovated, you are most likely in the lower half of the scale.

How to read the numbers, not just the letter

The letter is the headline, but the certificate includes two scales with specific figures that are worth checking:

  • Non-renewable primary energy consumption (in kWh/m² per year): how much “expensive” energy your home needs. The lower, the better.
  • CO2 emissions (in kg CO2/m² per year): how much it pollutes. This goes hand in hand with consumption.

And there is one section almost nobody looks at, but it is gold: the recommendations document. There, the technician includes the specific improvements that would raise your rating (changing windows, improving insulation, installing aerothermal heating, adding solar…), often with an estimate of the savings. It is a free mini-diagnosis of where to start if you want to improve your home.

How much it costs, how long it lasts and when it is mandatory

The practical side of the certificate

  1. Price — indicative, not regulated: between around €50 and €200 for a flat, more for detached houses. Includes the visit, report and registration. Be wary of very cheap certificates with no in-person visit.
  2. Validity — generally 10 years, but only 5 years if the rating is G. The worst rating expires sooner, to push owners to improve it.
  3. Mandatory — to sell or rent any home. Not having one can lead to fines of €300 to €6,000.
  4. Who issues it — a qualified technician after an in-person visit. You cannot do it yourself, and a certificate issued without visiting the property is not valid.

Why it matters more than ever in 2026

The certificate has gone from an annoying formality to a strategic document, for three reasons:

  • Tax deductions: as we saw when discussing deductions for solar self-consumption (autoconsumo solar) and efficiency, to access the most attractive ones (40% or 60% off IRPF) you need to prove the improvement with one certificate before the work and another after it. Without a certificate, there is no major deduction.
  • European regulation is tightening: the European Union has stated that the least efficient homes will have to improve in the coming years. A home rated F or G today will probably need renovation in the next few years to comply with the law.
  • The value of your home: according to data from Banco de España, homes with a good rating (A-B) sell at a significant premium compared with those rated F-G. The letter already affects the price.
The mindset shift: the certificate is no longer just a piece of paper needed to sell. It is a diagnosis of your home that determines how much you spend, which grants or deductions you can access and how much your property is worth. Improving the letter — with insulation, aerothermal heating or solar — has become an investment with returns through several routes at once.

How to improve your rating

If your home is in the lower half of the scale (E, F or G), the most effective improvements, ordered by impact, are usually: insulating the building envelope (the biggest factor), replacing windows, swapping old heating for aerothermal heating or a heat pump, and adding solar self-consumption. Many of these qualify for deductions and grants, so the real cost is lower than it seems — and the new certificate, with a better letter, increases the value of the home.

In summary

The energy performance certificate measures your home’s efficiency with a letter from A (green, best) to G (red, worst), plus two scales for consumption and emissions and a very useful recommendations section. It costs between €50 and €200, lasts 10 years (5 if it is G) and is mandatory to sell or rent. In 2026 it matters more than ever: it is the key to tax deductions, European regulation is starting to require the worst-rated homes to improve, and a good rating increases your home’s value. It is worth understanding it and, if your letter is low, considering how to improve it.
