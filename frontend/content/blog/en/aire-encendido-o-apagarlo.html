It’s the argument every summer: some people say you should leave the air conditioner on all day because “starting it up uses loads of energy”, while others say you should turn it off whenever you can. Like most household debates, both sides have a point and neither is completely right. The real answer depends on two things: what type of unit you have and how long you’ll be away.

First, understand how your air conditioner cools

The key is whether your unit is inverter or not — and almost all units sold in recent years are. That difference changes the answer completely:

Two very different ways of working

  1. Old air conditioner (non-inverter) — it runs at full power or switches off, like a light switch. It starts hard, cools, switches off, and then starts hard again. Every start-up costs energy.
  2. Modern air conditioner (inverter) — it adjusts its output like a car accelerator. It starts strong to reach the target temperature and then slows down to maintain it, using little energy.

That difference is everything. An inverter uses most of its energy during the initial start-up — when it has to bring the room temperature down from the outside heat — and very little to maintain it once it gets there. So what really uses energy isn’t “having it on”, but “cooling from scratch” again and again.

The answer if you have a modern air conditioner (inverter)

The way an inverter works gives us this practical rule:

Short absences (less than 1-2 hours): with an inverter unit, it’s usually better to leave it on at a stable temperature. Turning it off and back on shortly afterwards forces another strong start-up — the moment that uses the most energy — to recover the heat the room has gained. Maintaining costs less than recovering.
Long absences (half a day or more): turn it off. However efficient it is at maintaining temperature, that many hours of consumption eventually add up to more than the start-up you’ll need when you get back. There’s no point cooling an empty home all afternoon.

In other words: if you’re popping out to buy something, leave it on. If you’re going to work for eight hours, turn it off. The break-even point is somewhere between one and a couple of hours, depending on your unit and how well insulated your home is.

And if your air conditioner is old (non-inverter)

Here things change — and it matters, because the myth of “don’t turn it off, starting it uses a lot” comes precisely from these older units. And even with them, it isn’t entirely true.

A non-inverter unit doesn’t modulate: it always runs at full power and switches off only when it reaches the target temperature, then starts again at full power when the room warms up. In other words, even if you “leave it on”, internally it is constantly starting and stopping at full power. It doesn’t have the gentle maintenance mode that an inverter does.

With a non-inverter air conditioner: turn it off whenever you go out, even if it’s only for a short while. Since it doesn’t have an economical maintenance mode — it only knows full power or off — leaving it on while you’re away won’t save you anything: it will keep starting at full power every time the room heats up. The supposed “saving” from not turning it off is a myth with these units.

To sum up the difference between the two types:

What to do depending on your unit

  1. Inverter, short absence — leave it on and stable. Maintaining uses little energy; cooling from scratch uses a lot.
  2. Inverter, long absence — turn it off. So many hours maintaining the temperature end up costing more than the start-up when you return.
  3. Non-inverter, any absence — always turn it off. It has no efficient maintenance mode; leaving it on does not save energy.

And if you don’t know which one you have: units bought in the last decade are usually inverter (it will say so on the label or in the manual, and it’s what is sold today). If yours is already quite old and you notice it starts and stops abruptly with a noticeable “clunk”, it probably isn’t.

What really uses energy (and almost nobody checks)

More important than the on/off debate are two things that multiply consumption without you realising:

  • The temperature you set. Every degree lower pushes consumption up. Setting 22 ºC instead of 26 ºC on a hot day can noticeably increase energy use, because the unit has to work much harder against the difference with the outside temperature. In summer, 26 ºC is the recommended balance between comfort and cost.
  • Insulation. If cool air escapes through poorly closed windows or direct sun comes in because the blinds are up, your unit is cooling the street. Closing up and creating shade saves more than any on/off trick.
The most expensive mistake: setting the air conditioner to 18 ºC thinking “it will cool faster”. It won’t cool faster — the unit works at its own pace — it just makes it run at full power for longer and the room ends up too cold. Set it directly to the temperature you actually want.

In short

If your air conditioner is modern (inverter), leave it on for short absences — maintaining uses less energy than cooling from scratch — and turn it off for long absences. But the on/off debate matters less than the temperature you choose and your home’s insulation: that’s where most of the cost is. Set it to 26 ºC in summer, lower the blinds, close the windows, and the rest barely matters.
