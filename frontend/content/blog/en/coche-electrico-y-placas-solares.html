Of all the energy uses in a home, the electric car is the one that gets along best with solar panels. It is a large load, it is daily and —unlike the fridge or heating— you can choose fairly freely when to charge it. That flexibility is exactly what makes the car + solar combination so powerful.

The idea is simple: turn your car’s “tank” into the cheapest energy you can get, using the sun your roof is already producing.

Why they fit so well together

Solar panels have one “problem”: they produce at midday, which is exactly when many people are not at home to use that energy. That surplus is exported to the grid at a low price, and that is where part of the value of the system is lost.

The electric car solves this almost perfectly. It is a large load that you can schedule to charge precisely during sunny hours —or overnight, in off-peak (valle)— instead of at any random time. It turns the surplus you were going to give away to the grid into kilometres for your car.

The idea in one sentence: without a car, you sell your spare midday solar cheaply; with a car, you “drink” it yourself as kilometres. The same panel delivers more value.

The three ways to charge, from most expensive to almost free

How much it costs to fill the “tank” depending on how you charge

  1. Public fast charger — the most expensive option, mainly for trips. Convenient on the road, but the price per kWh is the highest.
  2. At home, at any time — at the normal price of your tariff. Cheaper than public charging, but not optimised.
  3. At home, during off-peak hours — charging overnight. The cheapest kWh from the grid: it can be half the price of peak (punta).
  4. At home, with your own panels — charging with the midday sun. Practically the lowest possible marginal cost: energy you already produce.

The difference between the first and the last is huge. Charging with your own solar production is, by far, the most economical way to run an electric car: you are refuelling with energy your roof generates anyway.

The effect on the profitability of your panels

Here is a detail many people miss: having an electric car improves the profitability of a solar installation, it does not just make the car cheaper to run.

The reason is self-consumption. A solar installation performs better the more of its production you use directly (instead of exporting it cheaply to the grid). An electric car adds a large, flexible load that you can line up with production hours, increasing that self-consumption percentage. The result: the panels pay for themselves sooner and the car costs less to charge. Both come out ahead.

That is why it is worth looking at them together: if you are thinking about an electric car and solar panels, the question is not “does each one pay off separately?”, but “how much better does everything get if I have both at the same time?”. Almost always, quite a lot.

What you need to make the most of it

You do not need anything exotic, but you do need a couple of things:

  • A home charging point that lets you schedule the time, so you can match charging with the sun or with the off-peak period.
  • Some schedule flexibility: the car has to be plugged in during production hours. If you use it during the day and do not get back until night, you will rely more on off-peak charging; if you work from home or the car spends the day at home, you can use direct solar.
  • A well-sized installation that includes the car’s extra consumption. An electric car adds a significant amount of annual demand, so the optimal number of panels changes if you have one.

When it really pays off

The combination shines if you drive a lot of kilometres (more charging = more savings), if you can charge during the day or schedule charging in off-peak hours, and if your roof is suitable for solar. If you barely use the car or can never charge it during cheap hours, the effect is smaller —although charging at home will always be cheaper than using a public charger—.

How to see it with your own numbers: if you have or are going to have an electric car, include it in the calculation when sizing your solar installation. The recommended size and payback period change quite a lot when you add that consumption.

In short

Electric cars and solar panels reinforce each other: the car uses the surplus the panels would produce anyway, and in return it helps the panels pay for themselves sooner. Charging with your own sunshine is the cheapest way to move a car that exists. If you are considering both, look at them together.
