If you’ve checked the electricity price this week, you’ll have noticed something striking: at night it’s sky-high, but around midday it drops sharply. On these July days, with the heat pushing demand up, the difference is huge: the night-time peak hour (hora punta) can cost more than four times as much as the cheap midday hour. It’s not a coincidence or a market whim. It’s solar energy setting the price in real time, and understanding why tells you exactly when it makes sense to consume.

Why the price collapses at midday

The electricity price is set every hour according to which energy sources are needed to meet demand. At midday, with the sun high in the sky, solar plants all over Spain feed a huge amount of electricity into the grid. That energy is so cheap to produce that it pushes the market price down: there is so much solar supply that expensive sources like gas are not needed.

As the afternoon fades and night arrives, the opposite happens: the sun goes down, solar production disappears, and that is exactly when people come home, switch on lights, air conditioning and appliances. High demand and little cheap supply: to cover it, gas plants have to be switched on, and they are expensive. The result: the price shoots up.

The pattern, in one sentence: when the sun is producing, electricity is cheap; when the sun goes down, electricity becomes more expensive. That is why the cheapest time of day is usually the middle of the day, and the most expensive is at night.

What we are seeing these July days

This hot week shows it in an extreme way. On a typical day like these, the electricity price draws a deep valley in the middle hours —when solar production is at full power— and two high peaks: one early in the morning and another, the most expensive of all, at night, when the sun disappears and air conditioning use rises.

The cheap slot is usually roughly between 12:00 and 16:00, and the expensive one between 20:00 and midnight. It can vary from day to day, but the pattern —cheap during the day, expensive at night— repeats because its cause, the sun, is always the same.

How to take advantage without panels

Even if you don’t have panels, you can benefit from this pattern simply by shifting your consumption:

  • Run heavy appliances at midday if you can: washing machine, dishwasher, tumble dryer. At the weekend it’s even easier.
  • Set your electric water heater to heat during the middle hours of the day, not at night.
  • Charge your electric car by taking advantage of that midday price dip when you’re at home.
  • Avoid concentrating consumption in the night-time slot, which is when you pay the most.

This is especially useful if you have an indexed tariff, where you follow the real hourly price. With a fixed tariff you pay the same at any time of day, but even then, with the 2.0TD tariff, the off-peak period (tramo valle) is still cheaper.

And why this is the best argument for self-consumption

Here is the interesting connection. If electricity is cheap at midday because there is a lot of sun, imagine having that sun on your own roof: instead of buying that cheap electricity from the grid, you produce it yourself and pay nothing for it.

And there is a double effect. When night arrives and the grid price shoots up, you have already consumed a good share of your solar energy during the day —directly or, if you have some flexibility, by concentrating your consumption then—. Self-consumption disconnects you precisely from the most expensive hours, because you produce exactly when the grid is cheap and reduce what you buy when it is expensive.

The useful irony: days like these, with high prices because of the heat, are exactly when a solar installation produces the most —there is plenty of sun— and when buying from the grid is most expensive. In other words, self-consumption shines exactly when everyone else’s bill shoots up.

In short

Electricity is cheap at midday because the sun floods the grid with low-cost energy, and expensive at night because, once the sun goes down, gas has to be used. You can take advantage by shifting your consumption to the middle hours, especially with an indexed tariff. And it is also the clearest argument in favour of self-consumption: producing your own energy when the sun is strong protects you from the most expensive hours.
