“My roof doesn’t face south, so solar panels won’t work for me.” It’s one of the most repeated lines when someone starts thinking about solar self-consumption, and it contains a myth worth debunking with data. Orientation matters, yes, but much less than most people think —except in one specific case—. Let’s look at real numbers for what happens depending on where your roof faces, and whether the dreaded north really is mission impossible.

First, why south is ideal

In Spain, as in the whole northern hemisphere, the sun traces its arc across the south: it rises in the east, reaches its highest point facing south, and sets in the west. That’s why a south-facing panel receives more direct sunlight and for more hours throughout the day. It’s the orientation that maximises annual production, which is why it’s used as the benchmark (100%) against which the others are compared.

The myth: “if it’s not south, it’s not worth it”

That’s where the mistake is. Many people think any deviation from south ruins the installation, and that’s nowhere near true. Here’s what actually happens by orientation:

How much each orientation produces (compared with south = 100%)

  1. South — 100%, the benchmark. Maximum annual production.
  2. South-east or south-west — around 95-98%. Deviating up to 45° from south only costs about 1-5%. Practically the same as south.
  3. Due east or due west — around 80-85%. You lose 10-20%, but it can still be perfectly profitable.
  4. North — 50-60% less than south. Here, yes: the loss is big. The only truly problematic case.

The conclusion is obvious: anything that isn’t north is viable. A south-east or south-west-facing roof produces almost the same as a south-facing one. And even a due east or due west-facing roof, which sounds “bad”, only loses 10-20% — a drop that can often be offset by adding one or two more panels, and that doesn’t stop the installation from being profitable.

The nuance that changes the decision: with an east or west orientation, you don’t just lose a little production; that production is distributed differently. East produces more in the morning and west in the afternoon. If your biggest consumption is during those hours (for example, if you get home in the afternoon), a west-facing orientation can even be used better than south, because it produces right when you consume it.

So, is north mission impossible?

Let’s be honest: north is, by far, the worst orientation, and in most cases it isn’t recommended. Losing more than half of the production means the installation takes much longer to pay for itself, and often it doesn’t make sense. If your only available surface faces north, the first step is to consider alternatives.

But “not recommended” isn’t exactly the same as “impossible”, and there are nuances:

  • If the roof is flat, the roof’s orientation doesn’t matter: the panels are installed on a tilted structure facing south, and that’s it. A “north-facing roof” is only a real problem on pitched roofs where the panels sit flush with the slope.
  • With a corrective mounting structure on a north-facing pitched roof, the angle can be improved, although it makes the installation more expensive and doesn’t always pay off.
  • If you have another surface —another side of the roof, a shed, the ground in the garden— it’s almost always better to use that than to insist on the north-facing side.
The honest answer: a pitched roof that only faces north is the one case where solar self-consumption really does get complicated, and it’s worth studying carefully or looking for alternatives. But if your roof faces east, west, south-east or south-west —or it’s flat— the idea that “I need a perfect south-facing roof” is just that: a myth. Your installation can be profitable.

What matters more than the exact orientation

One final note to put things into perspective. Once north has been ruled out, fine-tuning the orientation has much less impact on whether solar pays off for you than other factors: how much you pay for electricity, how much energy you use during the day (direct self-consumption), whether there are shadows from chimneys or trees —which can penalise production heavily— and the size of the installation. Obsessing over having an exact south-facing roof, when your roof faces south-west, means worrying about 3% while ignoring what really moves the numbers.

In short

South is the ideal orientation, but the myth that “without south it isn’t worth it” is false: south-east and south-west produce almost the same (95-98%), and east or west remain profitable (losing only 10-20%, sometimes offset by your consumption profile). The only truly problematic case is a pitched roof that only faces north, where you lose 50-60% —and even then, if the roof is flat or there’s another surface available, there’s a solution. Before ruling out solar because of orientation, look at what really matters: your bill, your daytime consumption and shading.

The best way to clear up any doubts is to check it with your own data: calculate your installation using your electricity bill and see whether it pays off with your real orientation.
