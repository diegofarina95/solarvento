It’s one of the things that most surprises people who have solar panels, or are thinking about installing them: if there’s a general power cut at midday, with brilliant sunshine and your panels producing at full capacity… will you have electricity at home? For a standard installation, the answer is no. Your home goes dark just like your neighbour’s house without panels. It sounds absurd, but there’s a safety reason behind it that makes complete sense once you understand it.

The short answer: it shuts down too

A standard self-consumption installation —solar panels connected to the grid, without a battery— stops working when there is a power cut, even during the day when the panels are getting plenty of sun. The moment the electricity grid goes down, your inverter detects that there is no grid and disconnects automatically. Your panels are still receiving sunlight, but that energy has nowhere to go: the system stops.

It’s hard to believe: you can be in the middle of a power cut, with your panels bathed in midday sun, and still be unable to switch on even a single light bulb. It’s not a fault or a defect: it’s the system working exactly as it should.

Why it shuts down: anti-islanding protection

The reason is safety, and it protects lives. When there is a power cut, the electricity company’s technicians go out to repair the grid, and they work on cables that are supposed to be dead.

Imagine your solar installation kept feeding electricity into the grid during the outage: you would be sending current into cables that a technician believes are dead. You could electrocute someone repairing the line. To prevent exactly that, the law requires all inverters to include anti-islanding protection: as soon as they detect that the grid has gone down, they disconnect automatically. It’s what stops your home from becoming a dangerous electrical “island” for the grid.

So your installation shuts down not because it has failed, but because it is protecting the people fixing the outage. It is a mandatory and sensible safety measure.

So how do you keep the lights on during a power cut?

You need specific equipment that most domestic installations do not include as standard. And this is where the most expensive and most common mistake comes in, so read this carefully:

Having batteries is NOT enough. This is the misunderstanding that costs many people dearly: they install several batteries, spend a fortune, and when the power cut arrives they are just as much in the dark as their neighbour. Why? Because batteries alone do not provide backup: they need a hybrid inverter (or an inverter with a backup function) capable of disconnecting from the grid and continuing to power your home in island mode. Without that inverter, batteries are only useful for storing energy and saving money day to day, but during a power cut the system still shuts down. Four batteries with a standard inverter leave you without electricity during the outage exactly as if you had none at all.

What you really need to have electricity during a power cut:

  • A hybrid inverter prepared for backup: this is the KEY component, even more than the batteries. It detects the power cut, safely isolates itself from the grid (complying with anti-islanding) and continues supplying your home from the batteries and panels. Without it, backup is not possible, no matter how many batteries you install.
  • Batteries with enough capacity to cover whatever you want to keep running during the outage. But they are the “tank”: without the hybrid inverter managing them in island mode, they do not kick in during a power cut.
  • Sometimes, a dedicated backup circuit: some systems only keep certain sockets or essential rooms powered during the outage, not the whole house. It’s worth knowing what will remain supplied.
Before you sign, here’s the question that saves you: “During a power cut, will this installation give me electricity, yes or no? Does it include a hybrid inverter with backup function?” Get them to confirm it IN WRITING. There have been cases of people installing several batteries believing they would have backup, only to lose power anyway when the first outage arrived — because the inverter was not hybrid. Don’t assume “battery = electricity during power cuts”: they are different things, and the inverter is what decides.

And is backup worth it?

It depends on where you live and how much it matters to you. If you live in an area with frequent outages, or if a power cut would create a serious problem for you (you work from home, have medical equipment, etc.), backup has real value. If power cuts where you live are extremely rare and brief, paying extra for that function may not be worth it — it is a peace-of-mind decision, not a savings decision.

The important thing is to go in with the right information: a standard solar installation, by itself, does not give you independence during a power cut. If that is what you want, it is a separate feature that you need to request and that costs more.

In summary

When there is a power cut, a normal solar installation also shuts down —even if it’s sunny— because of mandatory safety protection (anti-islanding) that prevents technicians repairing the grid from being electrocuted. To have electricity during an outage, having batteries is not enough: you need a hybrid inverter with backup function that can isolate your home from the grid and keep supplying it. That is the component that really matters, and the one many people discover they were missing on the day of the power cut, when it is already too late. If having electricity during outages matters to you, ask for a hybrid inverter with backup and get it confirmed in writing: not every installation provides it, even if it is packed with batteries.
