Of all the ways to lower your electricity bill, this is one of the easiest — and one that almost nobody checks: contracted capacity (potencia contratada). Many households pay every month for more capacity than they actually use, and adjusting it is free and shows up as a saving on every bill after that. Let’s see how much you really need.

What exactly is contracted capacity?

Contracted capacity, measured in kilowatts (kW), is the maximum amount of electricity you can use at the same time in your home. It is not about how much you consume in a month, but about how many high-power appliances you can run simultaneously without the breaker tripping.

Think of it like the width of a pipe: it is not how much water you use, but how much can flow through at any given moment. And here is the key to your bill: you pay a fixed fee for that “width” whether you use it or not. The more capacity you contract, the more you pay every month, whatever you do.

Why it matters: the capacity charge (término de potencia) is a fixed part of your bill that does not go down even if you consume less. It is one of the few fixed items you can actually act on, and doing it once saves you money on every future bill.

The sign that you’re overpaying

There is a very simple home test: if your power never — or almost never — “goes out” when you switch on several high-power appliances at once, you probably have more capacity than you need.

The breaker trips when you try to use more power than you have contracted. If you can have the oven, ceramic hob, microwave, and washing machine running at the same time without anything cutting out, you have spare headroom — and you are paying for that headroom every month.

How to calculate what you need

The capacity you need depends on which appliances you might use at the same time at the most demanding moment of the day. A practical way to estimate it is:

Approximate power of the most demanding appliances

  1. Oven — 2,000 to 2,500 W — one of the biggest instant loads. 2 to 2.5 kW on its own.
  2. Ceramic hob / induction — 2,000 to 3,000 W — depending on how many rings are on at once. One large ring is already over 1 kW.
  3. Electric water heater — 1,500 to 2,000 W — while it is heating. You can schedule it so it does not overlap with your busiest usage times.
  4. Washing machine / dishwasher — 1,500 to 2,200 W — especially when heating water. The peak is short, but high.
  5. Air conditioning — 1,000 to 2,000 W — depending on the unit. It adds up if you have several.
  6. Small loads (fridge, TV, lights, chargers) — together, a few hundred watts. The background load that is almost always active.

The idea is not to add up everything you own, but to think about the real most demanding moment: for example, cooking with the oven and hob while the washing machine is running. Add up the appliances that do actually overlap in practice and include some margin for smaller loads. For many households, between 3.45 and 5.75 kW is enough; only large homes, highly electrified homes, or households with several high-power appliances running simultaneously need more.

A sensible way to adjust: this is not about cutting capacity aggressively and causing outages. First, look at your day-to-day life: if the power has not gone out for months, not even when you are maxing things out (cooking with the oven and hob while something else is running), you have plenty of room to move down one step. A very occasional trip at the most demanding moment of the year is manageable; frequent trips are not — they are annoying and a sign you have gone too low. Adjust with a margin, not right on the limit.

How to change it

You request the capacity change through your electricity supplier, and it is a simple process. Keep a couple of things in mind: normally, you can only make one free reduction every so often (usually once a year without connection-rights costs), and lowering capacity has no technical cost, while increasing it again later may involve a small charge. That is why it is worth adjusting thoughtfully, not recklessly.

With the 2.0TD tariff, you can also have two different capacity levels: one for the peak/flat period (punta/llano) during the day and another for the off-peak period (valle) at night. If your heavy consumption is during the day, you can fine-tune it even more.

In short

Contracted capacity is the maximum you can use at the same time, and you pay a fixed fee for it whether you use it or not. If your power never goes out, you are probably overpaying: calculate which high-power appliances you really use at the same time, adjust to that level with a bit of margin, and enjoy the saving on every bill that follows. It is one of those changes you make once and keep saving from forever.

Reviewing your capacity is the first step; understanding the rest of your bill is the next one. And if, after optimising the free fixes, your bill is still high, it may be time to look at producing your own energy — but that is another story.
