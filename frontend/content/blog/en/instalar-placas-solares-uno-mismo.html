If you’re the kind of person who enjoys doing things with your own hands, at some point you’ve probably thought: “what if I install the panels myself and save on labor?”. It’s a tempting idea, and online you’ll find kits that look like you can simply assemble them and you’re done. But the honest answer, with Spanish law in hand, has important nuances you should understand before buying anything.

The short answer: it depends on what you mean by “install”

Here’s the key point that almost nobody explains properly. There are two different things: the physical installation (getting the panels onto the roof, fixing them in place, running the cables) and legalization (making sure the installation is legal, safe, and can be connected to the grid or qualify for grants).

  • The physical installation, in part, is something you can do yourself if you have the right knowledge. The law does not ban you from putting panels on your roof and securing them.
  • But legalization absolutely requires the signature of an authorized installer (instalador autorizado). There is no legal way around this if you want a grid-connected installation.
The point that changes everything: for your installation to be legal and for you to connect it to the grid, access surplus compensation (compensación de excedentes), or apply for subsidies, you need an Electrical Installation Certificate (the “boletín” or CIE), and that document can only be issued by an authorized electrical installer. No matter how handy you are, you cannot sign that certificate yourself.

Why the law requires it

This isn’t red tape for the sake of it. A solar PV installation handles electricity that connects to your home’s electrical system and may potentially export energy to the public grid. A mistake in the connections or protections can cause fires, electric shocks, or put technicians working on the grid at risk. That is why the regulations —the Low Voltage Electrotechnical Regulation (REBT) and Royal Decree 244/2019 on self-consumption— require a professional to certify that everything meets safety standards.

In other words: the installer’s signature is not an empty formality. It is a qualified person taking legal responsibility for the fact that your installation is not going to kill anyone or burn your house down.

The special case: off-grid installation (not connected to the grid)

There is an important nuance. If your installation is off-grid (aislada) —completely disconnected from the grid, typical of rural properties or homes without access to the electrical grid, with batteries for storage— the framework is somewhat more flexible, because you are not connecting to the public grid or exporting surplus energy.

Even so, be careful: even an off-grid installation must comply with the REBT and, depending on the power rating and the municipality, may require legalization, a responsible declaration, and registration in order to have insurance coverage and access grants. “Off-grid” does not mean “no rules at all”. And there is still a real electrical risk if it is done badly.

So what can you actually do yourself?

The realistic breakdown of DIY

  1. Design and sizing — research, calculate how many panels you need. You can and should: the more you know, the better your decisions will be.
  2. Buy the materials — choose panels, inverter, mounting structure. You can, although it is wise to get advice so everything is compatible.
  3. Part of the physical installation — structure and panel placement. Possible if you know what you are doing; roof work carries real risk.
  4. Electrical connection and protections — wiring, inverter, residual-current devices. This is where you are already in authorized-professional territory.
  5. Certificate, legalization, and registration — the legal paperwork. This must be done by an authorized installer.

Is it worth doing it yourself?

When you run the numbers, the savings from DIY are smaller than they may seem, for several reasons:

  • You will still need the installer anyway for the certificate and the connection, so you do not save all the labor cost, only part of it.
  • An installer buys materials more cheaply than you do (wholesale prices), which reduces the advantage of buying on your own.
  • A sizing or installation mistake can cost you more than you save: lower production, or having to redo the work.
  • Roof work carries a real risk of falls. This is not putting up a shelf.
The honest conclusion: you can get involved, learn, and do part of the work, and if you love technical DIY it can be very rewarding. But you are not going to “skip the installer” entirely, because legalization requires one. For most people, the real savings from doing it themselves do not outweigh the risk, time, and headaches.

In summary

In Spain, you can do part of the physical installation of your panels, but you cannot legalize a grid-connected installation without an authorized installer signing the certificate: the regulations require it for safety reasons. Off-grid installations are somewhat more flexible, but they are not free from rules either. And when you look at the numbers, the savings from DIY are usually lower than expected, because you still need the professional and they buy at better prices. Get involved, yes; bypass the law, no.

Before deciding anything, the first step is to know whether solar makes sense for you and what size system you need: calculate your case with your electricity bill and start from there with clear numbers.
