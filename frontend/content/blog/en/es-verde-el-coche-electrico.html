Few topics spark as much debate as this one: some say the electric car is the planet’s salvation, while others argue it pollutes more than a petrol car because of the battery. As is almost always the case, the truth sits at neither extreme. Manufacturing an EV does create more emissions upfront, but that is not the whole story. Let’s look at the data, without taking sides blindly.

What is true: manufacturing it pollutes more

Let’s start by giving the sceptics credit where they are right. Manufacturing an electric car emits more CO2 than manufacturing a petrol car, mainly because of the battery: lithium, nickel and cobalt mining, plus the process of making the cells, have a considerable footprint.

How much more? Studies put it at around 30% more emissions during manufacturing for an electric car compared with an equivalent combustion car. It is a real difference and should not be hidden: an electric car leaves the factory with a heavier carbon “backpack” than its petrol rival.

So anyone saying this is not lying: a newly manufactured electric car has emitted more CO2 than a newly manufactured petrol car. If the story ended at the factory gate, the sceptics would be right. But it does not end there.

What gets forgotten: use changes everything

Manufacturing is only the beginning. A car spends most of its life on the road, and that is where the tables turn completely. A petrol car burns fuel every kilometre and emits from the exhaust throughout its life. An electric car emits nothing while driving; its only emissions come from the electricity you charge it with, which depends on the energy mix.

As the miles add up, that advantage in use starts to offset the initial “backpack” from manufacturing. The key question is not “which one pollutes more when it leaves the factory?” (the electric car), but “after how many miles has the electric car offset its initial disadvantage?”.

The break-even point: when does it start to pay off?

This is where the studies put numbers on it, and they are more favourable than many people think:

Mileage after which the electric car pollutes less

  1. ICCT study (International Council on Clean Transportation) — the electric car breaks even after around 17,000 km. Between one and two years of normal driving.
  2. Volvo study (European mix) — break-even point at around 77,000 km. It depends heavily on the electricity you charge with.
  3. Charging with 100% renewable energy — the break-even point falls to around 49,000 km or less. The cleaner the electricity, the sooner it pays off.
  4. Duke study (PLOS Climate) — matches the footprint in barely two years of use. From then on, every km is a net saving.

Studies vary depending on the country, the electricity mix and the assumptions used, which is why the figures range from 17,000 to 77,000 km. But they all agree on the essential point: within a relatively short period —between one and a few years of normal use— the electric car has already offset its higher manufacturing footprint, and from that point on every kilometre is a net emissions reduction compared with petrol.

The decisive factor: what electricity you charge with

The real footprint of an electric car is not a fixed number: it depends enormously on the electricity it uses. If you charge it in a country that relies heavily on coal, its advantage shrinks. If you charge it in Spain —where the mix already exceeds 50% renewables— the advantage is significant. And if you charge it with your own solar panels, use-phase emissions get close to zero: in that case, the only major footprint is manufacturing, which is paid back very quickly.

The ultimate combination: an electric car charged with your own solar energy is, in carbon-footprint terms, almost unbeatable. Manufacturing still has a cost, but driving becomes almost emission-free. That is where the “green car” goes from slogan to measurable reality.

The honest answer

Is the electric car green? Honestly: it is not zero-emission —manufacturing it pollutes, and charging it does too if the electricity is dirty— but over its full lifetime it emits considerably less than a petrol car. Life-cycle studies are consistent on this, even when the battery is included. The phrase “it pollutes more because of the battery” is only true if you stop at the factory gate and ignore the years of driving that come afterwards.

It is not a perfect technology or one without impacts —materials mining is a real problem that needs to improve— but in the overall balance, and especially when charged with clean electricity, it pollutes less. That is the answer supported by the data, without hype and without myths.

In summary

Manufacturing an electric car emits around 30% more CO2 than a petrol car because of the battery. But because it does not emit while driving, it offsets that disadvantage between 17,000 and 77,000 km depending on the study and the electricity mix —in other words, within a few years— and from then on it pollutes less for the rest of its life. Charged with renewable energy, and even more so with your own panels, the advantage grows sharply. It is not zero-emission, but across the full balance it is cleaner. That is the honest answer.
