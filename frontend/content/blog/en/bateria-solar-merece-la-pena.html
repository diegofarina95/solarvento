A battery is, at the same time, what an installer will try hardest to sell you and what usually makes the least sense on pure profitability in Spain today. Not because it’s bad technology, but because the numbers still don’t add up in most cases. Let’s do the real calculation, without the brochure enthusiasm.

Let’s be clear from the start: this is not “batteries are useless”. It’s “before paying several thousand euros for one, check whether it makes sense in your specific case, because very often it doesn’t”.

What a battery actually does

Without a battery, the solar energy you produce and don’t consume at that moment is exported to the grid, and you’re compensated for it at a low price. With a battery, you store that midday surplus to use it at night, instead of buying it from the grid.

The value of the battery is therefore the difference between what you would have been paid for exporting that surplus (not much) and what it would have cost you to buy that energy at night (more). That difference per kWh is what the battery “saves” you. And that’s the problem: today, that difference is usually not enough to pay for the battery within its useful life.

The calculation, in approximate figures

Why the numbers are tight

  1. Cost of a home battery — several thousand euros depending on capacity. As a guide: around €500–800/kWh installed.
  2. Useful life — around 10–15 years. Current LFP warranties are roughly in that range.
  3. Typical payback — it often exceeds 10 years. Dangerously close to the end of its useful life.
  4. The savings ceiling — it can only save you money on the surplus you currently export cheaply. If you don’t have surplus energy, the battery has nothing to store.

The key point is the third one: if a battery takes almost as long to pay for itself as it lasts, it leaves you hardly any benefit before it needs replacing. In the best case, you “break even”; you rarely make much. That’s why, today and on pure profitability, the battery is the weakest link in a solar self-consumption installation.

The industry’s common mistake: calculating the battery payback as if it were part of the whole system. The right approach is the incremental payback: how much does the battery cost SEPARATELY and how much does it save SEPARATELY? When you split it out like that, the uncomfortable truth almost always appears: it takes too long to pay for itself.

When a battery DOES make sense

It’s not all about pure profitability. There are cases where a battery can be justified, as long as you go in with your eyes open:

  • You value energy independence above return on investment: you want to depend less on the grid, have backup during outages, or it simply matters to you. That’s a legitimate decision, but it’s a preference, not a profitability argument.
  • You have high night-time consumption that solar can’t cover during the day: here the battery works harder and the numbers improve, although it rarely becomes clearly profitable.
  • You live in an area with frequent power cuts and backup has real value for you.
  • Subsidies cover a significant part of the cost: if a grant pays for a good chunk of the battery, the calculation changes. Check the calls for applications in your autonomous community.

When it DOESN’T

If your motivation is purely economic —“I want it to pay off”— and you don’t meet any of the conditions above, the battery most likely won’t be worth it for you today. Start with solar panels only, which usually are profitable, and leave the battery for later: prices are still falling and in a few years the calculation may be different. You don’t have to decide everything now.

A practical tip: if an installer gives you a quote with a battery included “as standard”, ask them to break down the numbers WITH and WITHOUT the battery separately. You’ll see the real difference in payback, and you’ll be able to decide for yourself, not let them decide for you.

In short

A solar battery in Spain today rarely pays for itself within its useful life: its payback often comes dangerously close to the end of its lifespan. It makes sense if you value independence, have high night-time consumption or get a good subsidy, but not as an investment that quickly pays for itself. If you’re unsure, start with solar panels and leave the battery until prices fall further.
