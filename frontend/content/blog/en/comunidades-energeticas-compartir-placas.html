Not everyone can put solar panels on their roof: maybe you live in a flat, your roof is shaded, or you simply don’t have enough space. But that no longer leaves you out of solar energy. Sharing an installation with nearby neighbours or businesses — what is known as shared self-consumption (autoconsumo compartido) and energy communities — is becoming increasingly viable in Spain, and in 2026 the legal framework has become much more favourable. Here’s the plain-English version.

The idea: you don’t need your own roof

The concept is simple: instead of everyone installing their own panels, a group of people or businesses shares the same solar installation and divides up the energy it produces. The panels can be on the roof of a residential building, on a nearby industrial unit, or on any suitable roof in the area. Each participant receives a share of the production, which is deducted from their electricity bill.

This opens solar up to many people who were previously left out: anyone living in an apartment block, anyone without their own roof, or anyone whose roof has a poor orientation. Instead of giving up on self-consumption, they join a shared scheme.

The big change in 2026: the rules have increased the maximum distance between the panels and the people who consume their energy. Previously, participants had to be very close to the installation; under the new framework, that radius has expanded significantly (up to around 5 km in a straight line under the most recent rule), which greatly increases the possibilities for shared projects in neighbourhoods, municipalities and business parks.

Watch the jargon: not everything is the same

This is where almost everyone gets confused, because similar terms are used for different things. Let’s clear them up:

The three concepts people mix up

  1. Collective self-consumption (or shared self-consumption) — several consumers share the same installation and divide up its energy. This is the technical setup: how the electricity is allocated.
  2. Solar community — a project where a company promotes the installation and local residents benefit through a subscription, often without investing directly. A business model for easy access.
  3. Energy community — a legal entity in its own right (cooperative, association) where members take part in generation, management and more. The broader and more participatory model.

In practice, for an ordinary resident, the key point is that you can share solar energy in different ways: by financing your share of a common installation, or simply by subscribing to a project that already exists. The legal details are handled by whoever promotes or coordinates the project.

How the energy is shared out

Each participant is assigned an allocation coefficient: the percentage of production that belongs to them. This allocation can be fixed or dynamic (adjusting according to each person’s consumption throughout the day), and it is officially registered. The energy allocated to you is deducted from your bill as if it were your own, and surplus energy compensation (compensación de excedentes) can work in the same way as for an individual installation.

One important detail: because the energy travels through the distribution grid, small grid access charges (peajes) apply for its use. Even so, they are far lower than buying that electricity directly from the market, so the savings are still real.

The advantages of sharing

  • Access without your own roof: you can use solar even if you live in a flat or your roof is not suitable.
  • Shared investment: by splitting the cost between several people, your initial contribution is lower.
  • Better performance: one large, well-oriented shared installation will usually produce more effectively than many small, poorly located ones.
  • The same incentives: surplus compensation and public grants also apply to these projects.

And the obstacles, honestly

It is not all straightforward. Setting up a shared project has its complexities: several participants need to agree, the paperwork and administrative steps are more involved than for an individual installation, and coordinating the allocation and billing requires management. That is exactly why the 2026 rules have created the role of the self-consumption manager (gestor de autoconsumo), a person or entity that represents the participants and takes care of the paperwork and coordination, reducing that friction.

In practical terms: if you are interested but the management side feels overwhelming, the usual route is to join an existing project (a solar or energy community in your area) rather than setting one up from scratch. That way, you get the benefits without dealing with all the bureaucracy.

In short

Energy communities and shared self-consumption let you enjoy solar energy even if you do not have your own roof: you share an installation with nearby neighbours or businesses and split the production between you. With the wider radius in 2026 and the new self-consumption manager role, setting up or joining these projects is more viable than ever. They are not bureaucracy-free, but they open the door to solar for people who were previously left out.

If you have your own roof and first want to see what an individual installation could give you, calculate your case with your bill. And if you do not, you now know that sharing is becoming a more realistic option every year.
