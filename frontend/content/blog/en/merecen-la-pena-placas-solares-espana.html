“Are solar panels worth it?” is the right question, but almost nobody answers it honestly. The real answer is “it depends”, and it depends on very specific things you can check yourself. Let’s look at them without the hype: when solar really pays off and when it’s better not to get into it.

Spain has a starting advantage that’s hard to beat: lots of sun. But sunshine is only one of the factors, and not always the deciding one. A good roof in Galicia can be more profitable than a poorly oriented one in Almería. These are the four factors that really matter.

The four factors that decide

What determines whether it pays off for you

  1. How much you consume — the more electricity you use, the more you save with each panel. Very low consumption makes the payback period much longer.
  2. When you consume — solar produces during the day; if you use electricity during the day, you use it directly. Mostly night-time consumption = less benefit without a battery.
  3. Your roof — orientation (south is best), tilt and shading. A north-facing or heavily shaded roof takes quite a hit.
  4. The price you pay for electricity — the more expensive grid electricity is, the more each kWh you avoid buying is worth. Expensive tariffs speed up the payback period.

When it DOES pay off (almost certainly)

The ideal case brings together several of these conditions:

  • Medium or high consumption — say, above roughly 3,500–4,000 kWh per year — especially if you have air conditioning, a heat pump, a pool or an electric car.
  • A good share of your consumption during the day: working from home, someone at home, or appliances running during sunlight hours.
  • A south-, south-east- or south-west-facing roof, with little shade and some available space.
  • Your main home, not a second home you use two months a year.

In a case like this, typical payback periods in Spain are around 5–8 years, while panels last 25–30. For the rest of their life, it’s savings.

The key isn’t covering 100%: the most profitable system is usually not the one that covers all your consumption, but a slightly smaller one that you use almost entirely. Oversizing just so you “don’t buy anything from the grid” means exporting lots of cheap surplus and lengthening the payback period. Fewer well-used panels perform better than lots of poorly used ones.

When it DOESN’T pay off (or you should think twice)

Here’s the part almost no installer will tell you upfront:

  • Low consumption — below roughly 2,000–2,500 kWh/year: the annual savings are so small that the installation takes a decade or more to pay for itself. Sometimes that money works harder elsewhere.
  • Almost all your consumption is at night and you don’t plan to install a battery: you produce during the day what you don’t use and export it cheaply, while you keep buying expensive electricity at night.
  • A second home used only occasionally: you pay for the whole installation but only take advantage of the sun for a few weeks a year.
  • A north-facing, heavily shaded or space-limited roof: production falls so much that the numbers stop adding up.
  • You have the social bonus (bono social) or another heavily subsidised tariff: if your electricity is already very cheap, the saving per avoided kWh is small and the payback period shoots up.
And the battery is a separate matter: a battery almost never “pays for itself” based on profitability alone in Spain today — its payback period often exceeds 10 years, close to its useful life. It makes sense if you value independence or have a lot of night-time consumption, but don’t include it in the calculation expecting it to pay for itself quickly.

The mistake of trusting the average

You’ll see plenty of headlines like “solar pays for itself in 6 years in Spain”. That may be true on average and false for you. Your real payback period depends on the exact combination of your four factors: two neighbours with the same roof but different consumption and usage patterns can have payback periods that differ by years.

That’s why the only serious way to answer “does it pay off for me?” isn’t to read an average, but to enter your own data: your real consumption (from your bill), your exact location (for the solar radiation in your area) and your roof.

How to get a clear answer with your own numbers: upload your bill to the calculator and work out your specific case — estimated production using PVGIS data for your location, real savings and years to payback. And if it turns out solar doesn’t pay off for you, it’s better to know before spending anything.

In short

Solar pays off in Spain when you consume a fair amount, a good share of it during the day, you have a decent roof and you pay a normal electricity price. It doesn’t pay off — or you need to think carefully — if you consume little, almost all at night, you have the social bonus or your roof is poorly oriented. And the optimal system is almost never the biggest one, but the one you use almost entirely.

The average doesn’t decide for you. Your data does. Calculate your case with your own bill and clear up your doubts with real numbers, not headlines.
