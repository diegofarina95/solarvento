There is one type of solar installation that almost nobody talks about and yet it is one of the most profitable there is: the one that covers a building’s shared electricity use. The lift, the entrance and stair lighting, the garage door, the water pump… all of that uses electricity during the day, exactly when the sun is producing. That overlap is what makes installing solar panels for the common areas of an apartment building such a strong economic choice.

Why common areas are the ideal case

The big limitation of solar in a home is that you produce at midday, when often there is nobody at home to use it. With a building’s common areas, the opposite happens: their consumption is daytime-based and constant. The lift goes up and down all day, the garage lights and systems are always active, and the water pump can run at any time. That consumption matches solar production hours almost perfectly.

What does that mean? That direct self-consumption (autoconsumo directo) is very high: almost all the energy produced by the panels is used on the spot, instead of being exported cheaply to the grid. And as we have explained in other articles, high self-consumption is exactly what makes an installation pay for itself quickly. In common areas, that self-consumption is almost handed to you.

The financial key: the savings from this installation go straight to the community fee. A lower electricity bill for common areas means lower shared expenses to divide among residents, month after month. It is a collective saving that everyone notices.

What the building’s solar can power

Shared consumption that solar panels can cover

  1. Lift — one of the most constant shared electricity uses. It runs all day, so it fits solar production perfectly.
  2. Entrance, stair and garage lighting — permanent lighting in many areas. Especially the garage, which is often lit all the time.
  3. Garage door, pumps and motors — small loads that add up. Automatic door, water pressure system, ventilation.
  4. Swimming pool filtration or irrigation — if the community has them, they run during the day. The filtration system operates exactly during sunny hours in summer.
  5. Shared EV chargers — the real gem: charging with surplus energy. The midday surplus can feed charging points.

Shared EV chargers: making use of surplus energy

This is the most interesting and newest part. A common-area installation sized with a little margin produces more at midday than the lift and lights consume. Instead of exporting that surplus to the grid for a low payment, the community can use it to power electric car charging points in the garage.

The result is powerful: residents with electric cars can charge during the day almost entirely with solar energy from their own building, at a minimal cost, while the surplus that would otherwise be almost given away to the grid is turned into kilometres. It is a way to give value to every kWh produced and share the benefits among more residents.

The legal side: what majority is needed at the owners’ meeting

This is what everyone worries about, and the good news is that the law has made it straightforward. For an installation intended for the building’s common areas, Spain’s Horizontal Property Act, Ley de Propiedad Horizontal (article 17.2), requires:

Simple majority: approval only requires the favourable vote of a simple majority of owners who also represent a simple majority of the ownership shares —provided that the annual cost of the installation, after deducting grants, does not exceed the equivalent of 12 ordinary monthly payments of common expenses—. Unanimity is not required. And once approved, both the investment and the savings are shared by everyone.

This is much more accessible than the unanimity many residents fear. But do not confuse the cases: if a small group of residents wanted panels for their own homes (private use), the required majority is different (one third). For common use by the building, however, a simple majority is enough, and those who vote against it also benefit from the savings and contribute to the cost.

The steps, in short

  • Bring the proposal to the owners’ meeting with quotes, and include it on the agenda in advance.
  • Approve it with the simple majority under article 17.2.
  • Process the municipal licence or responsible declaration, and the self-consumption register (registro de autoconsumo).
  • Install (the work is usually quick, just a few days) and legalise it with the certificate from the authorised installer.
  • Activate surplus compensation (compensación de excedentes) if the installation generates surplus energy.
A management tip: the biggest barrier is usually not technical or financial, but getting residents to agree. Bringing clear numbers to the meeting —how much is saved each year on the community fee, and how quickly the system pays for itself— is far more convincing than talking about sustainability in the abstract. With the argument “this lowers the community fee”, the votes often follow naturally.

In short

Installing solar panels for the common areas of an apartment building is one of the most profitable solar uses there is, because the consumption (lift, lighting, garage) happens during the day and matches solar production, pushing self-consumption up sharply. The savings reduce everyone’s community fee, and surplus energy can be used to power car chargers. Legally, a simple majority at the owners’ meeting is enough (article 17.2 LPH) if the cost does not exceed 12 monthly payments of common expenses. The main challenge is getting agreement, and for that, clear numbers are the best argument.
