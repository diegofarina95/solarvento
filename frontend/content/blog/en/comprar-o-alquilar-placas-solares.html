Until recently, there was only one way to get solar panels: buy them. Today, the market offers several alternatives —renting, leasing, subscription, PPA— that promise solar energy without having to pay thousands of euros upfront. They sound convenient, and in some cases they are. But the small print can change the numbers a lot, so it is worth understanding each option properly before signing a contract that may last twenty years. Let’s compare them without buying into anyone’s sales pitch.

The options, from most to least “yours”

First, let’s clarify the terms, because they are often used confusingly and they do not all mean the same thing:

The ways to get solar panels

  1. Outright purchase — you pay for the installation and it is yours from day one. Maximum long-term savings; requires an upfront payment.
  2. Financed purchase — you buy it, but pay in instalments through a loan. It is yours. No large upfront payment, but with interest; you are still the owner.
  3. Leasing — rental with an option to buy at the end. It can end up being yours. A middle ground; at the end you decide whether to keep it.
  4. Renting — pure rental, with maintenance included. Normally it is NOT yours. Fixed all-inclusive monthly fee, but you are not the owner.
  5. PPA (power purchase agreement) — you are not renting the panels: you buy the energy they produce at an agreed price. The company installs and owns the system; you only pay for the electricity it generates.

Why buying usually wins in the long run

The logic is the same as buying versus renting a home: when you rent, part of what you pay goes towards the profit of the person or company renting it to you. The same happens with solar panels. With renting or rental, much of the saving you achieve on your electricity bill goes into the monthly fee, because that fee has to cover the installation plus the company’s margin. Your real net saving ends up being small.

With buying, on the other hand, once you have paid back the installation —usually in 5 to 8 years— the rest of its useful life (up to 25-30 years) is pure saving for you. That is the big prize renting does not give you: years of free savings after payback.

The calculation almost nobody does: with a 20-year rental or renting contract, you usually end up paying much more than the cost of buying the installation in the first place —and at the end, in many contracts, the panels are not even yours—. You pay for two decades to use something you could have bought and paid off in less than half that time.

The decisive factor people often forget: tax incentives

This is the most important point and the one that is mentioned least, so underline it: if you are not the owner of the installation, you cannot access subsidies or tax deductions.

Self-consumption grants, the personal income tax deduction through IRPF and the IBI rebates offered by many town councils are designed for whoever invests in their own installation. In a renting contract or a PPA, because the installation belongs to the company, those benefits stay with the company (or simply do not apply to your case). Giving up the incentives can mean losing a very significant part of the saving, because in many cases they cover a large chunk of the cost. Very often, this is the argument that tips the balance towards buying.

Other downsides of renting worth knowing

  • Locked into a long contract: renting contracts often last up to 20 years, with penalties if you want to leave early.
  • A problem when selling your home: if you sell the property with an active renting contract, the new owner has to take on the monthly fee, which can complicate the sale.
  • It does not increase your home’s value as something you own: an owned installation adds value to the property; a rented one does not in the same way.
  • Dependence on the company: you remain tied to the provider throughout the contract, for better or worse.

So when does renting make sense?

Rental or renting is not a scam: it has its place. It may make sense if:

  • You cannot or do not want to take on the initial investment or apply for financing, and you prefer a fixed all-inclusive monthly fee.
  • You place a high value on convenience and not having to worry about anything: maintenance, breakdowns and management are handled by the company.
  • You do not pay enough tax to make use of the tax deductions, so you lose less by not being the owner.
  • It is a temporary solution or you are not sure how long you will stay in that home.
The rule of thumb: if you can afford to buy (even with financing), buying almost always works out more profitable in the long run and gives you access to incentives. Renting mainly makes sense as an entry route for people who cannot invest upfront and prioritise convenience and a fixed monthly fee over maximum profitability.

A middle-ground alternative: buying with financing

Many people rule out buying because they think it means paying everything upfront, but that is not the case. A financed purchase lets you be the owner from day one —with access to grants and deductions— while paying in instalments, just as you would pay a renting fee. You usually end up paying less than with an equivalent renting contract, and the installation is yours. For many households, it is the sweet spot: no large upfront payment, but with the advantages of ownership.

In short

Buying solar panels —either upfront or with financing— is usually the most profitable option in the long run: you pay them back in 5-8 years and then enjoy years of pure savings, as well as access to subsidies and tax deductions that renting does not allow. Renting, leasing or a PPA avoid the initial investment and remove maintenance worries, but in exchange for a lower net saving, long contracts and, above all, the loss of incentives because you are not the owner. If you can buy (even in instalments), it normally pays off; renting is the route for those who prioritise not investing and convenience over profitability.

Before deciding how to pay for it, the first step is to know whether solar makes sense for you and what size your installation would be: calculate your case using your bill and start from there with clear numbers.
