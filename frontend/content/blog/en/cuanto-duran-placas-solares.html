When someone starts thinking about installing solar panels, sooner or later the big question comes up: I’m going to invest several thousand euros in something they say lasts 25 years… is that really true? And what exactly happens the day those 25 years are up — do the panels suddenly switch off? The answer is more reassuring than many people think, but there’s an important detail that almost nobody explains properly. Let’s look at the real data.

The “25 years” are not an expiry date

First and most important: the 25 years you hear everywhere are not the point when the panels stop working, but the period usually covered by performance warranties. When they reach that age, panels don’t switch off or break down: they keep producing, just a little less than on day one. In fact, a quality installation can continue generating useful energy for 30, 35 or even 40 years.

The key is understanding that a solar panel doesn’t “die”: it degrades very gradually. It’s not an on/off switch; it’s a very gentle slope.

What degradation is — and why it’s good news

Degradation is the loss of performance a panel experiences each year. Today’s quality panels degrade very little: between 0.3% and 0.7% per year. That means each year they produce that tiny fraction less than the year before.

What a panel has left as the years go by

  1. At the start — 100% of its capacity. Nominal production in the first year.
  2. After 10-12 years — around 90%. You’ve barely noticed the difference.
  3. After 25 years — around 80-90%, depending on quality. The typical warranty guarantees at least 80%.
  4. After 30 years and beyond — a good panel can still be around 85%. It keeps producing and saving you money, even after the warranty has expired.

Let’s put that into a concrete example: if your installation produces 6,000 kWh in the first year, after 25 years it will still be generating around 4,800-5,400 kWh. In other words, it still covers the vast majority of what it covered at the beginning. And by then it will have paid for itself many years earlier, so all that energy is pure savings.

The reassuring fact: as proof that this technology really does last, the world’s oldest solar panel has been working for more than 60 years. Photovoltaic technology has no moving parts to wear out, which makes it extremely durable and prone to very few faults.

The caveat almost nobody tells you about: the inverter

Here’s the part many salespeople gloss over, and it’s worth knowing before you invest. Panels last 25-30 years or more, but the inverter — the device that converts the energy from the panels into the electricity your home uses — lasts less: between 10 and 15 years. That means that, over the lifetime of your installation, you should normally expect to replace the inverter at least once.

It’s not an unpleasant surprise if you know it in advance: it’s the only significant foreseeable cost over the system’s lifetime, and it needs to be included in profitability calculations if they’re going to be honest. A payback calculation that doesn’t include inverter replacement is painting too rosy a picture.

Watch out when comparing quotes: an honest calculator or installer includes inverter replacement in the long-term figures. If they show you a 25-year return without mentioning that the inverter will need replacing along the way, be wary: real calculations factor it in. Microinverters with a longer lifespan (15-25 years) do exist as an alternative, although they come with a different cost.

What affects whether they last more or less

  • Panel quality: high-end panels (Tier 1) degrade less (0.3-0.4% per year) and last longer than cheaper ones.
  • Climate: extreme heat speeds up degradation (panels perform worse above 25°C), and humidity or sea salt can cause corrosion. The Spanish climate is generally favourable.
  • Shade and dirt: they don’t “kill” the panel, but they do make it produce less; occasional cleaning helps.
  • Installation quality: a professional installation, securely mounted and properly wired, extends the life of the whole system.

So what should I do after 25 years?

When that moment comes, with the installation paid off long before, you have options — and none of them involve throwing everything away: you can keep using it as it is (it produces a bit less, but it still produces and saves money), replace only what’s needed (perhaps the inverter again, or the odd individual panel), or upgrade the modules to newer, more efficient ones if you want to get the maximum out of your roof. The mounting structure and wiring, if installed properly, usually last as long as the panels.

In short

Solar panels really do last: 25-30 years of guaranteed useful life and often quite a bit more, degrading very gradually (0.3-0.7% per year) until they’re at around 80-90% of their capacity after 25 years. They don’t switch off when the warranty ends; they keep saving you money. The only component you’ll need to replace is the inverter (every 10-15 years), and that needs to be included in the figures for them to be honest. Once that detail is accounted for, a solar installation is one of the most durable home investments you can make.
